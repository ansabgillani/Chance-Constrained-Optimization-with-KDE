\section{Discussion and Practical Guidance}
\label{ch:discussion}

The experimental evidence supports a conditional method choice. KDE is useful when uncertainty is empirical, residual dimension is low, and a smooth constraint is needed by the optimizer. The results do not support treating every low test frequency as a safety certificate. Solver status, estimator identity, bandwidth, sample protocol, and validation scope determine the claim that can be made.

\subsection{Schuster--Keil crosswalk}

The two anchor papers answer different questions. Schuster studies convergence of optimization problems as the KDE approximation improves. Keil constructs a biased integrated kernel and embeds it in a sampling and optimal-control procedure. The local implementation tests transferable structures and records the missing conditions in Table~\ref{tab:discussion-crosswalk}.

This crosswalk explains why the local findings do not contradict the literature. A lower biased-KDE test frequency is compatible with Keil's one-sided construction. A nonmonotone finite-sample objective is compatible with Schuster's asymptotic theory because no monotone finite-$N$ objective law is asserted. The local sequence is evidence about implementation behaviour under declared choices.

The local results favour KDE for exploratory and engineering problems where smoothness and distributional flexibility matter. They do not justify KDE when a finite-sample certificate is the primary requirement or when the residual dimension makes density estimation unstable.

The appropriate reporting unit is therefore formulation, estimator, bandwidth, training sample, solver status, held-out frequency, and validation scope. The conclusion is concise because the tables carry the comparison: use scenario methods for certification, parametric reformulations when their assumptions are credible, unbiased KDE for smooth empirical modelling, and biased KDE only when its one-sided construction and sampling conditions are actually implemented. The thesis evidence supports that rule and identifies the experiments required before stronger claims are warranted.
